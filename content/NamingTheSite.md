# "Penny Press", and why the site is called Newsprint instead

Surfaced 2026-09-05, when the name changed. Kept because the history is good
and the argument is sound — it is simply an argument about the half of this
design that matters least. No front matter, so `bin/build-content` skips it:
this is a working note, not a piece (SPEC §10.1).

**Short version: the *New York Sun* is the right parallel for metering's
pricing and the wrong name for a site whose thesis is unobserved reading.**

## The parallel, which is a real one

The *New York Sun*, 3 September 1833: one cent a copy, sold in the street by
newsboys, against established papers at six cents and sold by annual
subscription. It is the closest historical parallel there is to what metering
proposes — unbundling a subscription into a price per item, at a price low
enough that the decision stops being a decision.

That last clause is the part worth keeping. A cent is not a small subscription;
it is a different kind of transaction, because nobody deliberates over a cent.
Metering is interesting for the same reason the Sun was: not that it was
cheaper, but that it moved the decision from *do I want a relationship with
this publisher* to *do I want this*.

## Where the analogy stops, and the objection it invites

**The analogy is to the unbundling and stops there.** It is not an argument
about how those papers were funded, and the site never made one.

Penny papers did carry advertising, and someone eventually raises it, so the
answer was written down rather than improvised: advertising in 1833 was
classified notices in a four-page sheet. It was not audience measurement, not
behavioural targeting, and not the sale of a reader's identity, none of which
existed for another century and a half. Drawing a line from one to the other
compresses a hundred and ninety years into a single claim and gets the history
wrong in order to sound neat.

An earlier draft went further and argued that penny papers were ad-funded, "so
this is the same fork in the road". That was dropped for two reasons, and both
still hold. The thesis is that per-view revenue is *priced to equal or better*
targeted-ad revenue — see `MeteredPayEconomics.md`, which finds the claim
defensible at some prices and not at a cent — and that thesis is orthogonal to
the name. And the argument made the name less attractive rather than more, by
attaching a contestable historical claim to it.

## Why the name changed anyway

The rebuttal above is sound and it should never have been load-bearing. It
existed to defend the name against an objection that the name itself invited,
which is a poor trade for a word on a masthead.

More to the point, the parallel is about **price**, and price is the less
interesting half of what this demonstrates. What the site actually argues is in
SPEC §10.2 and in `privacy.md`: that a reader can pay for what they read
without the site accumulating a record of what they read.

*Newsprint* carries that. You bought a paper, and nobody knew which parts of it
you read — not the newsagent, not the publisher, not an advertiser. Nobody
designed that property; it was a property of paper, and every digital
replacement has quietly removed it.

A name that states the thesis needs no paragraph defending it. That is the
whole of the case for the change.
